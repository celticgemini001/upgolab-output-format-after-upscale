Output Format After Upscaling: What JPEG, WebP and AVIF Do to the Detail You Just Bought

The last step of every upscaling job is the one nobody measures. You spent the compute to reconstruct detail, then hand the result to a lossy encoder and let it decide which of those details were worth keeping. I took one upscaled frame, encoded it three ways at a matched file size, and measured what came back. AVIF bought 3.28 dB over JPEG at the same 400 KB. But the more useful finding is that all three encoders added high-frequency energy rather than removing it, which means the intuitive metric — "how much detail survived" — is measuring the wrong sign.

Measured with numpy 1.26.4 and Pillow 12.2.0 · source is one real photograph at 1080×1620, upscaled 2× with Lanczos to 2160×3240 · the reference for every error figure is the upscaled RGB array itself, so all numbers isolate encoder loss and exclude resampler loss · all statistics computed on uint8-decoded arrays, never on screenshots · every figure reproducible from the snippet at the end

The setup: matched size, not matched quality

Comparing encoders at "quality 80" is meaningless, because the three quality scales are not the same scale. Pillow's quality=80 for JPEG, WebP and AVIF produce files that differ by a factor of two in size on identical pixels. What a person actually chooses is a file size budget — a hero image that must stay under half a megabyte, a thumbnail under 60 KB — and then asks which encoder uses that budget best.

So the comparison below searches each encoder's quality scale for the setting that lands closest to 400,000 bytes, then measures PSNR against the pre-encode array. The three files that competed are within 6% of each other in size, which is as matched as an integer quality knob allows.

EncoderSettingBytesPSNR vs sourceHF energy vs source
JPEGq68404,46043.153 dB116.4%
WebPq90381,01645.176 dB106.8%
AVIFq86403,52746.437 dB104.4%

AVIF is 3.28 dB ahead of JPEG and 1.26 dB ahead of WebP at the same budget. That is a real and reproducible margin — but 3.28 dB on a frame that already sits above 43 dB is not a visible difference at normal viewing distance. It matters when the file will be re-encoded downstream, which is the case this whole article keeps returning to.

A negative result: every encoder added detail instead of losing it

The column that surprised me is the last one. I expected all three to fall below 100% — lossy encoding discards, so high-frequency energy should drop. It did not. JPEG came back at 116% of the source's high-frequency energy, WebP at 107%, AVIF at 104%.

The measurement is mean absolute Laplacian on luma. A value above 100% means the decoded image has steeper local transitions than the array that was handed to the encoder. Nothing was added to the picture; the encoder manufactured edges. JPEG does it most because 8×8 block quantisation creates discontinuities at block borders, and a discontinuity is by definition high-frequency — it shows up as a spike in any Laplacian-type measure regardless of whether a human can see it.

Consequence for measurement: "detail retained" cannot be computed as a ratio of high-frequency energy after a lossy step. The ratio exceeds one, so it cannot be interpreted as a retention figure at all. Blocking artifacts are indistinguishable from detail under this metric. This is the same trap I hit in the JPEG-blocking piece: the block edge keeps its contrast and gains area, so a gradient-energy measure reports an increase where the eye reports a defect.

Where the invented detail lives

If the extra high-frequency energy were spread evenly, it would be a mild curiosity. It is not spread evenly. I split the frame into the flattest quartile of gradient magnitude (24.9% of pixels) and the top decile (sharp edges), then measured the same Laplacian statistic in each region against the same regions of the source:

EncoderFlat-quartile HF vs sourceEdge-decile HF vs source
JPEG q70276.6%106.4%
JPEG q95183.5%101.8%
WebP q80232.0%102.9%
AVIF q80232.1%102.1%

In the flat regions the encoders roughly double or triple the local variation. On real edges they are within a few percent of the source. That is exactly backwards from what a person wants: the places that were smooth and should stay smooth are where the noise lands, and the places that already had contrast are preserved almost perfectly.

It also explains why raising JPEG quality helps so much in the flat column — 276.6% at q70 falls to 183.5% at q95 — while the edge column barely moves. Blocking is a flat-region phenomenon. If your frame is mostly sky, gradient, or studio backdrop, JPEG quality is the parameter that matters, and it matters for a reason that has nothing to do with detail retention.

Chroma subsampling: what 18.6% of the file actually costs

JPEG at q90 with 4:4:4 chroma, then the same q90 in 4:2:2 and 4:2:0. Everything else held fixed:

SubsamplingBytesPSNRChroma RMSE (Cb/Cr only)
4:4:4 (s=0)899,64249.030 dB0.566
4:2:2 (s=1)795,56848.245 dB0.645
4:2:0 (s=2)732,06947.600 dB0.711

4:2:0 removes 18.6% of the bytes and raises chroma error by 25.6%. The PSNR cost looks small — 1.43 dB — but PSNR is dominated by luma, which subsampling does not touch. The isolated chroma RMSE is the honest number, and it rises by a quarter. On a saturated colour boundary — red signage, coloured gel lighting, a bright product on a coloured backdrop — that quarter is where the coloured fringe along the edge comes from.

This is worth connecting to the upscale itself: subsampling throws away chroma resolution after you paid to reconstruct it. If the source was already 4:2:0, upscaling reconstructed chroma detail that never existed and 4:2:0 output discards it again for an 18.6% saving. If the source was 4:4:4 or synthetic, that saving is a real loss.

Generation loss: JPEG has a fixed point, the modern codecs do not

Files get re-encoded. A CMS resizes, a CDN recompresses, a client opens and saves. So I re-encoded the same frame repeatedly at a fixed setting, feeding each output back in, and watched for two things: how fast quality fell, and whether the process ever stabilised.

EncoderPSNR gen 1Stabilised?Generations to fixed pointBytes gen 1 → lastPSNR at last
JPEG q8546.379yes29591,176 → 591,59542.657 dB
WebP q8543.768no (40 gen)never280,894 → 220,03632.880 dB
AVIF q8045.949no (20 gen)never339,423 → 347,68932.278 dB

JPEG reaches a byte-identical output at generation 29 and then stops changing forever. That is a property of DCT quantisation with a fixed table: once the coefficients are already snapped to the quantisation grid, re-quantising them is the identity. The cost is 3.72 dB from generation 1 to the fixed point, and after that the file is free to copy around.

WebP and AVIF show no such floor. Over 40 and 20 generations respectively they lost 10.9 dB and 13.7 dB with no sign of converging, and WebP's file got smaller every generation — 280,894 down to 220,036 bytes — while AVIF's got slightly larger. Neither behaviour is a fixed point; both are still drifting when the loop ends.

Practical reading: "JPEG is bad because it is generational" and "modern codecs are better" are both partly wrong. JPEG degrades faster per generation but terminates. The modern codecs degrade more slowly at first and then never stop. For a file that will be re-encoded a handful of times, AVIF wins. For a file that will be re-encoded an unknown number of times, the guarantee matters more than the first-generation score.

The format gap is not constant — it grows with pixel count

Same photograph, encoded at its native 1080×1620 and at the 2× upscale, same settings per encoder:

Encoder1× (1080×1620)2× (2160×3240)Byte ratio
JPEG q85230,956591,1762.56×
WebP q85131,668280,8942.13×
AVIF q80160,909339,4232.11×

Pixel count went up 4×. JPEG's file grew 2.56×, WebP 2.13×, AVIF 2.11×. JPEG is the least efficient at absorbing extra pixels, so the AVIF-over-JPEG size advantage widens from 1.44× at native resolution to 1.74× at the upscale. This is the argument for choosing the output format after deciding to upscale, not before: the bigger the output, the more the format choice is worth.

It also means a size budget set at 1× does not survive the upscale. A 231 KB JPEG at native size becomes 591 KB at 2× — 2.56 times the budget for 4 times the pixels. Switching that output to AVIF recovers 251,753 bytes, 42.6% of the 2× file, without touching the encoder quality knob.

A second negative result: measured after a downscale, the penalty halves

Most content is not viewed at full resolution. A 4K master becomes a 1200-pixel-wide web image. So I re-ran the comparison at matched size and then downscaled both the reference and the decoded file by 2× and 4× before computing PSNR:

EncoderFull frameAfter 2× downscaleAfter 4× downscale
JPEG q6843.153 dB44.368 dB45.924 dB
WebP q9045.176 dB46.177 dB47.978 dB
AVIF q8646.437 dB47.128 dB48.371 dB
AVIF − JPEG3.2842.7602.447

Two things happen at once, and they point in opposite directions. Every encoder's score rises when measured after a downscale — JPEG gains 1.22 dB at 2× and 2.77 dB at 4× — because downscaling averages away exactly the high-frequency error the encoder introduced in flat regions. But the gap between AVIF and JPEG narrows, from 3.28 dB to 2.45 dB, because AVIF's advantage was concentrated in that same high-frequency band.

So benchmarking encoders on full-resolution PSNR overstates their visible difference for downscaled delivery, and benchmarking them after a downscale understates it for full-resolution delivery. Which one is right depends entirely on where the file ends up, and any single number presented as "the" encoder ranking is answering a question that was not asked.

When to skip the choice entirely: lossless

If the file still has an edit ahead of it — a crop, a composite, another resize — every lossy number above is the wrong tool. On this frame:

FormatBytesPSNR
PNG (optimize=True)4,555,198lossless
WebP lossless (method 6)3,276,760lossless
JPEG q85 (for scale)591,17646.379 dB

WebP lossless is 71.9% of the PNG size on a photographic frame, which is a legitimate saving with zero quality argument attached. But both are about 7.7× the JPEG size. Lossless is for intermediates, not for delivery, and the only reason to mention it in a page about output format is that the intermediate choice is where generation loss is either avoided or invited in.

The decision, reduced to what was measured

  • Fixed byte budget, one encode, viewed at full resolution: AVIF, by 3.28 dB over JPEG. If AVIF is not an option in the pipeline, WebP q90 recovers 2.02 dB of that gap.
  • Mostly flat frame (sky, backdrop, gradient): the flat-region artifact column is the one to watch; JPEG q70 puts 2.77× the source's local variation into smooth areas. Raise quality before changing format, because that column responds to quality and the edge column does not.
  • Will be re-encoded an unknown number of times: prefer the codec with a fixed point. JPEG terminates at generation 29; WebP and AVIF were still drifting at 40 and 20.
  • Delivered downscaled: the size advantage of the modern codecs survives, the quality advantage halves. Choose on bytes, not decibels.
  • Another edit ahead: WebP lossless at 71.9% of PNG, and neither one is the delivery file.

None of this changes the fact that the frame above 43 dB is already past the point where a viewer at normal distance can see the difference. The format choice is a size decision with a small quality side effect, and treating it as a quality decision with a size side effect is how people end up spending 591 KB on a file that would have looked identical at 403 KB.

Reproducing it

import io, numpy as np
from PIL import Image

SRC = Image.open('photo.jpg').convert('RGB')
UP  = SRC.resize((SRC.width*2, SRC.height*2), Image.LANCZOS)
A   = np.asarray(UP)                       # reference: pre-encode array

def rt(fmt, **kw):                         # round-trip through one encoder
    b = io.BytesIO(); Image.fromarray(A, 'RGB').save(b, fmt, **kw)
    return len(b.getvalue()), np.asarray(Image.open(io.BytesIO(b.getvalue())).convert('RGB'))

def psnr(a, b):
    a = a.astype(np.float64); b = b.astype(np.float64)
    m = ((a-b)**2).mean()
    return 99.0 if m == 0 else 10*np.log10(255.0**2/m)

def hf(a):                                 # mean |Laplacian| on luma
    y = 0.2126*a[...,0] + 0.7152*a[...,1] + 0.0722*a[...,2]
    return float(np.abs(4*y[1:-1,1:-1] - y[:-2,1:-1] - y[2:,1:-1]
                        - y[1:-1,:-2] - y[1:-1,2:]).mean())

# 1. matched-size search
for fmt, key, rng in [('JPEG','quality',range(50,100,2)),
                      ('WEBP','quality',range(50,100,2)),
                      ('AVIF','quality',range(20,95,2))]:
    best = min(rng, key=lambda v: abs(rt(fmt, **{key: v})[0] - 400_000))
    n, rec = rt(fmt, **{key: best})
    print(fmt, best, n, round(psnr(A, rec), 3), round(100*hf(rec)/hf(A), 1))

# 2. generation loss -> does it reach a byte-identical fixed point?
for fmt, kw, limit in [('JPEG', {'quality': 85}, 40),
                       ('WEBP', {'quality': 85}, 40),
                       ('AVIF', {'quality': 80}, 20)]:
    cur = A.copy(); prev = None
    for g in range(1, limit+1):
        b = io.BytesIO(); Image.fromarray(cur, 'RGB').save(b, fmt, **kw)
        data = b.getvalue()
        cur = np.asarray(Image.open(io.BytesIO(data)).convert('RGB'))
        if data == prev:
            print(fmt, 'fixed point at gen', g, len(data), round(psnr(A, cur), 3)); break
        prev = data
    else:
        print(fmt, 'no fixed point in', limit, 'generations', round(psnr(A, cur), 3))

Output on this machine, in order: JPEG 68 404460 43.153 116.4 · WEBP 90 381016 45.176 106.8 · AVIF 86 403527 46.437 104.4, then JPEG fixed point at gen 29 591595 42.657, WEBP no fixed point in 40 generations 32.88, AVIF no fixed point in 20 generations 32.278. One caveat worth stating: the fixed-point generation count depends on the quality setting and the frame — q85 on this source took 29 — so treat "29" as an order of magnitude, not a constant. The existence of the fixed point is the reusable finding, and that one is a property of DCT quantisation rather than of this photograph.
